How to Clean Copper Plumbing Pipes: A Middletown, NJ Homeowner Guide

Cleaning copper plumbing pipes in a Middletown, NJ home means two different jobs: brightening the outside of exposed pipes and removing scale or buildup from fittings and fixtures. Both are simple, but neither should be done on a pipe that is leaking or corroded through.

Turn off the water first, and never use abrasive pads or harsh acids on a pipe that carries drinking water without rinsing well.

Outside versus inside

The outside of a copper pipe darkens and turns green or brown with time. That film is called patina, and it is a normal surface reaction, not leakage. Cleaning it is cosmetic.

The inside of a pipe collects mineral scale where water is hard. You cannot clean the inside of a whole house line yourself, but you can clean aerators, showerheads and valve fittings that scale up.

Brighten exposed copper

Mix equal parts white vinegar and water, or use lemon juice with a pinch of salt. Wipe the pipe with a soft cloth soaked in the mix and leave it for a few minutes. Rinse with clean water and dry with a towel.

For stubborn dark spots, use fine steel wool or a non-scratch scouring pad lightly. Rub along the pipe, not across it. Do not scrub hard on thin or old pipe.

Seeing pinholes or green crust that returns? Call a plumber.

Remove scale from fixtures

Soak aerators and showerheads in white vinegar for an hour, then scrub with a soft brush. Rinse before you reattach them. For a faucet with stuck scale, wrap a vinegar-soaked cloth around the spout for an hour.

Descaler products made for plumbing fixtures work too. Read the label, check that the product is safe for your finish and ventilate the room.

What not to do

Do not use drain cleaner, bleach or strong acids on copper supply pipes. They can eat into the pipe or react with the pipe's film. Do not mix cleaners. Do not use a wire brush on soft or thin copper.

Never pour acid into a drain that serves copper pipe. If you want to remove heavy scale inside a pipe, a plumber should decide whether the pipe is still sound.

When cleaning is the wrong answer

Call a plumber instead if you see

  • Green or white crust on a joint that stays damp.
  • A pinhole that sprays or weeps.
  • Blue-green stains around a fitting or in a sink.
  • Pipes that have turned flaky or pitted.
  • Water pressure that has dropped at several fixtures.
  • Repeated leaks in the same house.

Those signs point to corrosion or a failing joint, not a surface stain. Our leak detection page explains how a plumber finds the source, and repiping covers whole-house options when copper is wearing out.

Cleaning the finish before a sale or remodel

Exposed copper in a basement or utility room looks neater when it is clean, and clean copper makes it easier to spot new leaks. Before you start, dry the pipe and look for wet spots. Wipe, rinse and dry, then apply a thin coat of clear lacquer if you want the shine to last. Lacquer is a cosmetic choice and not a repair.

If the pipe will be soldered, the plumber cleans the end with an abrasive pad and flux. That step is for a new joint and is different from general cleaning.

Hot water lines versus cold water lines

Hot water lines wear faster than cold ones. Heat speeds up chemical reactions, so a hot line may show more discoloration and scale. Cold lines can sweat in humid weather and drip onto surfaces below, which looks like a leak but is condensation. Wrapping cold lines in insulation stops the drip. If the dampness is under a joint instead of along the pipe, treat it as a leak.

Local note

Middletown local note

New Jersey American Water's Coastal North system reported total hardness of 72 to 140 ppm in its 2023 report, so scale is normal on fixtures in Middletown. Scale itself is not a sign that your pipes are failing.

Plumbing work in New Jersey must be done by a licensed master plumber, and the state adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code 2021 at N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15. If you plan to replace sections of copper, ask the Middletown construction office at 1 Kings Highway, 732-615-2106, whether a permit applies. Red Bank has its own water source, covered on our Red Bank page.

A simple yearly routine

Wipe visible copper twice a year. Clean aerators and showerheads every few months. Look at joints and valves while you do, and note any damp spots. A quick look is how most small leaks are found before they cost money.
