Install a Water Powered Sump Pump: A Middletown, NJ Homeowner Guide

A water powered sump pump is a backup pump that uses your home water pressure instead of electricity, so it keeps working in a power outage. It suits some Middletown, NJ basements, but it uses a lot of water and needs good pressure and local approval.

This guide explains how it works, what an install involves and how to compare it with a battery backup.

How it works

A water powered backup pump sits in the sump pit next to the main pump. It connects to a pressurized cold water line. When the water in the pit rises past the main pump's level, the float triggers the backup. City water flows through a venturi, which creates suction that pulls pit water out through the discharge pipe.

The pump has no motor and no battery. It needs water pressure to run.

What it needs

Requirements for a water powered backup

  • A municipal water supply with steady pressure, not a private well.
  • A cold water line near the sump pit.
  • A discharge pipe that can carry both pit water and the supply water.
  • A check valve to stop backflow into the pit.
  • A backflow preventer on the supply line where required.
  • Approval from the local water authority and, where needed, a permit.

A well system with a weak pump cannot supply enough water for this design.

For a short, heavy storm, the water powered unit can be a good fit. For a long outage with lots of pumping, water use adds up. Our sump pump service page explains battery and water-powered options.

Installation overview

A plumber installs the unit above or beside the sump pit, runs a supply line from a cold water source, ties the discharge into the existing pipe and adds a check valve. A backflow preventer on the supply side protects the drinking water.

Then the plumber tests the system by raising the float and watching the discharge. Test it yourself twice a year, too.

Where the water goes

Discharge should go outside, away from the foundation. Do not connect sump discharge to the sanitary sewer, since it overloads the sewer and may be prohibited. Ask the authority what rules apply. If basement flooding repeats, ask about drainage as well. See basement waterproofing.

Water use in real terms

A water powered pump uses municipal water to move pit water, and the ratio can be steep. Many models use several gallons of supply water for every gallon they move, though the ratio depends on pressure and design. A long run during a major storm can use a large amount of water and show up on the next bill.

That is why most plumbers treat the water powered pump as a backup for short outages, not as a main pump. Ask for the manufacturer's data on water use at your supply pressure so you can estimate the cost.

Common problems

Things that stop a water powered backup

  • Low supply pressure, which weakens the suction.
  • A clogged venturi from mineral scale or debris.
  • A stuck float that never triggers the unit.
  • A discharge pipe that is blocked or frozen.
  • A closed or failing supply valve.
  • A check valve that has failed and lets water flow back.

Testing twice a year finds most of these while the weather is dry. Flush the unit during a test to clear debris.

Backflow protection

Because the pump connects to the drinking water supply, a backflow preventer keeps pit water from flowing back into the house plumbing. Local rules often require one. Ask the water utility or the construction office what device they accept and how often it must be tested.

Local note

Middletown local note

Homes on public water in Middletown are served by New Jersey American Water's Coastal North system. Ask the utility whether a water powered backup is allowed on your service and whether a backflow preventer is required. Properties with a private well generally cannot use this design.

Plumbing work in New Jersey must be done by a licensed master plumber, and the Middletown construction office at 1 Kings Highway, 732-615-2106, can confirm permit rules. Red Bank operates its own water system, so check the rules on our Red Bank page.

Plumbing standards in New Jersey come from the National Standard Plumbing Code 2021, adopted at N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15, and the licensed master plumber you hire is responsible for meeting them.

Test before storm season

Pour water into the pit, confirm the main pump starts and then confirm the backup operates when the float is lifted. Keep the discharge clear of leaves and ice.
